Most facial menus get built by looking sideways. You check what the spa two blocks over charges, land a few dollars under it, and print the list. Six months later you are booked solid and quietly resentful, because that number never accounted for your room rent, your product cost, or the ninety minutes a proper acne treatment actually takes.

Price from your cost, your time, and your skill. Not from a competitor's sign. That is the whole argument of this guide, and everything below is the arithmetic that backs it up. The spa down the street pays different rent, buys a different product line, and serves a different client, so their menu tells you very little about what yours should say. It is a sanity check, not an answer.

What follows is a working toolkit for [estheticians](/for-estheticians/) and new spa owners: real 2026 ranges by facial type so you know which neighborhood you are in, the cost-plus math that sets your own number inside it, the add-ons and upcharges that carry the margin a base facial cannot, a deposit structure that protects a ninety-minute room, and the short path from a finished menu to a live booking page. The ranges are useful on any platform you happen to run. At the end, we show how each line of your menu becomes a bookable service carrying its own price and deposit.

## What facials actually cost in 2026, by type.

Start with the map. These are the ranges most independent estheticians and small spas are charging in the US right now, from budget suburban markets at the low end to premium metro studios at the high end.

| Treatment | Typical range | Time | What moves the number |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Express facial** | $45-$75 | 30 min | Volume play, thin margin |
| **Classic / signature facial** | $75-$130 | 60 min | Your anchor price |
| **Deep-cleansing / acne facial** | $90-$150 | 60-75 min | Extraction time |
| **Dermaplaning** | $75-$150 | 45-60 min | Blade cost, training |
| **Microdermabrasion** | $100-$175 | 45-60 min | Equipment payback |
| **Chemical peel** | $100-$200 | 30-60 min | Acid cost, depth, liability |
| **Hydrafacial-style treatment** | $150-$300 | 45-60 min | Device lease, tip cost |
| **LED session (standalone)** | $30-$60 | 20-30 min | Near-zero consumables |

Read the spread carefully, because it is mostly market and product cost, not skill. A classic facial at $130 in a dense metro and the same facial at $80 in a small town are frequently performed by estheticians of identical ability. Rent, disposable income, and what a device costs to keep running explain almost all of the gap.

Two practical uses for this table. First, if your price sits below the bottom of a range, you are almost certainly undercharging, and no amount of being busy will fix that. Second, if you want to sit at the top of a range, the price has to be visibly earned somewhere the client can perceive: the intake, the room, the products, the finish. Nobody pays $180 for a peel because you said so. They pay it because the whole experience reads like $180.

## The cost-plus math behind every price.

A range is a neighborhood. Cost-plus gives you the address. The formula is short enough to do on the back of an intake form:

**Product cost + room cost for the time slot + your hourly labor target = your floor. Add margin on top. That is your price.**

Work a real one. A 60-minute signature facial: cleanser, exfoliant, mask, serum, moisturizer, plus disposables, runs about $12 per treatment for a mid-tier professional line. Your room costs $15 for that hour, whether you rent a treatment room at $1,200 a month or pay booth rent by the week. Your labor target is $60 an hour, which is what you have decided your hands are worth. Floor: $87.

Now add margin. At 30 to 40 percent over the floor you land at $113 to $122, so you post $115 or $125. That margin is not greed. It absorbs the sanitizing time between clients, the cancellations, the product that expires before it is used, and the slow week in February. Price at the floor and every one of those things comes out of your own pay.

The part most menus get wrong is time. Because your cost is dominated by the slot, a 90-minute facial has to cost meaningfully more than a 60-minute one, and two express facials in the same hour must together beat what a single classic facial brings in. If they do not, the express service is quietly cannibalizing your best hours. Run the arithmetic per slot, not per service, and you will spot the loss leaders on your own menu within about ten minutes.

## Add-ons and upcharges that hold your margin.

Here is the uncomfortable truth about a base facial: after product, room, and your hourly target, it often clears very little. The add-ons are where the real money is, which is exactly why they belong on the menu as priced line items and not as generous extras you throw in because the client was nice.

Give away extractions and LED all year and you have effectively cut your own prices by 20 to 30 percent without ever changing the menu. Post them, price them, and let clients choose. Most say yes when the option is presented as part of the plan for their skin.

## Series and memberships: pricing repeat skin work.

Skin does not change in one appointment. Acne protocols, pigmentation work, and peel courses need four to six visits spaced two to four weeks apart, so pricing them one session at a time works against the treatment itself and against your calendar.

Price the series. A six-treatment peel or acne course at 10 to 15 percent off the single-session rate is the right shape. On a $140 peel, that is roughly $714 to $756 for six instead of $840, and in exchange you get the whole course committed and often prepaid. A 12 percent discount for guaranteed, scheduled revenue is a trade worth making. Push past 20 percent and you are just training clients to wait for the bundle.

Memberships smooth the rest. One facial a month at a modest discount, plus a retail credit of $10 or $15 to use in the studio, gives you a predictable base of bookings and a reason for members to buy their home care from you instead of a drugstore. Members also rebook without being chased, which is worth more than the discount costs.

Both models need the money to actually move. Take prepayment at booking through [Stripe-backed payments](/platform/tools/payments/) so a series is paid for when it is scheduled, not collected appointment by appointment while you hope nobody drifts off after visit three.

## Put a deposit on the long ones.

A no-show costs a barber twenty minutes. It costs you an hour and a half of a reserved room, product you already decanted, and a slot somebody on your waitlist would have taken. Facials are exactly the kind of appointment deposits were invented for.

A structure that works: 30 percent on standard facials and single peels, and 50 percent on multi-hour treatments and the first appointment of a series, where the loss is largest and the client is least established. Deposits apply to the service total, so nobody is paying extra. They are just paying earlier.

Show the deposit on the menu, next to the price, before anyone reaches checkout. Deposits do not upset clients. Surprises upset clients. When the amount is visible at the moment they pick the service, it reads as a normal booking policy rather than a demand. Pair it with a clear cancellation window, 24 or 48 hours, and enforce it the same way for everyone.

The mechanics matter: a deposit only works if it is charged automatically at booking through [payments built into the booking flow](/platform/tools/payments/), not requested by DM afterward. ## How to raise your facial prices without losing clients.

If the math above says your menu is 20 percent low, do not fix it overnight. Fix it deliberately.

Give notice. Three to four weeks is enough for regulars to hear it twice, once in the room and once by email or text, and to book one more appointment at the old rate if they want to. Nobody should discover your new price at checkout.

Move in steps. Five to ten percent at a time, once or twice a year, is absorbed almost invisibly. A $110 facial going to $120 is a conversation nobody has. That same facial jumping to $145 in one move is a conversation everybody has, and some of them have it with a different esthetician.

Lead with value, not apology. Do not open with "unfortunately" or explain your rent. Say what the client is getting: your continuing education, the new product line, single-use everything, the results in their own before-and-after photos. Confidence in the delivery does most of the work.

And accept the small amount of churn. A handful of people leave over any increase. They are, nearly always, the clients who booked the cheapest service, never bought an add-on, and rescheduled twice. The regulars who trust your hands stay. Losing the bottom of your book while your average ticket rises is not a loss.

## Your price menu belongs on your booking page.

You can do all of this arithmetic correctly and still lose the booking, if the finished menu lives in a PDF, a highlight reel, or a number you quote when somebody messages you.

Every step between "what does a peel cost" and a confirmed appointment leaks clients. A price list that requires a DM adds a step and a wait. A PDF adds a download and pinch-zooming on a phone. Meanwhile the studio across town has the number, the duration, and the deposit sitting right next to a Book button.

The menu should live where the booking happens, with the price and deposit attached to the exact service being selected. Intake questions belong there too, since a first-time peel client and a monthly member need different questions before they arrive. That is what [per-service booking forms](/platform/booking/booking-forms/) are for. ## Questions

### How much should an esthetician charge for a facial in 2026?

It depends on the treatment and your market. A classic facial commonly runs $75 to $130, a chemical peel $100 to $200, and a hydrafacial-style treatment $150 to $300. Use those ranges as a sanity check, then set your actual number from your product cost, your room cost, and your hourly target.

### How do I calculate the right price for a facial?

Use cost-plus. Add your product cost per treatment, the room or booth cost for the time slot, and your hourly labor target, then add margin on top. A 60-minute facial with $12 of product, $15 of room, and a $60 hourly target lands near $110 to $130 at a healthy margin.

### Should I charge separately for add-ons like LED or dermaplaning?

Yes. Price them as line items, usually $20 to $45 each, rather than throwing them in for free. The base facial often runs close to break-even once product and room time are counted, so the add-ons are where your actual margin lives.

### Do I need a deposit for facial appointments?

For anything longer than a quick express treatment, yes. Facials run 45 to 90 minutes with the room reserved and product pre-set, so a no-show costs you real money. A 30 percent deposit at booking cuts no-shows sharply without scaring off good clients.

### How should I price a facial series or membership?

Offer a six-treatment series at 10 to 15 percent off the single-session price, or a monthly membership with one facial plus a retail credit. Skin results need repeat visits anyway, so pricing for the series matches how the treatment actually works.

### How do I raise my facial prices without losing clients?

Give a few weeks of notice, raise in small steps of 5 to 10 percent rather than one big jump, and lead with the value: your training, your sanitation, your product quality, your results. Most regulars stay. The ones who leave over a fair increase were usually your lowest-margin clients.

### Where should my facial price list live?

On your booking page, where the price and the deposit are attached to the exact service a client is booking. A PDF menu or a price you only quote in DMs adds a step, and every extra step loses bookings.
