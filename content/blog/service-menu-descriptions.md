A service menu has one job: to get a client to book the right thing without messaging you first. Every "which one do I book?" DM is a menu that did not do its job, and every client who books a fill when they needed a full set is an appointment that starts with a problem.

The fix is rarely more services. It is plainer names and a few short lines under each one that answer the questions a client would otherwise have to ask.

:::answer
Name each service the way a client would say it, then describe it in three or four short lines: what is included, how long it takes, who it is for, and what it is not. Keep the price and the length visible on every one. A client who can see all of that picks the right service the first time and turns up expecting what you are about to do.
:::

## Name it the way a client would say it

Clients book with the words they use, not the words on your training certificate. "Classic Lash Fill" is clear. "Signature Lash Refresh Experience" is a guess.

A few rules for names:

- **Use the plain word first.** "Skin fade", "brow lamination", "root touch-up". If you have a name you love, put it second: "Root touch-up (The Quick Refresh)".
- **One service, one name.** If a fill and a refill are the same thing, pick one word and use it everywhere: menu, confirmation, aftercare, your own texts.
- **Put the difference in the name.** "Volume full set" and "Volume fill" are two services a client can tell apart at a glance. "Volume" and "Volume II" are not.

## The four lines under every service

Under the name, answer four questions. Short lines, not a paragraph.

1. **What is included.** The parts of the appointment a client would ask about. A cut includes a wash or it does not. A full set includes a consultation and a patch test or it does not.
2. **How long it takes.** The real time, including the parts the client is sitting there for. If your menu says 60 minutes and the appointment always runs 80, the client has planned their day wrong.
3. **Who it is for.** The one line that stops the wrong booking. "For fills within three weeks of your last set." "For short hair that is already faded."
4. **What it is not.** Optional, and the most useful line on the menu when it applies. "Not for a first visit with me." "Does not include a beard line-up."

Keep the price on every one. A menu that hides price behind "from" or "ask" makes the client message you, which is the thing you were trying to stop. If a service genuinely varies, give the range and say what moves it.

## The template

:::copy Service description template
[Plain service name]
[Price] · about [length]
Includes: [what is part of the appointment].
For: [who should book this].
Not for: [who should book something else, and what that is].
:::

## Two filled examples

These are worked examples with round numbers, to show the shape. Use your own prices and times.

**A lash fill.**

:::copy Lash fill example
Classic lash fill
$65 · about 1 hour
Includes: removing grown-out extensions and filling in your set with classic lashes.
For: clients with at least half of their set still on, within three weeks of the last appointment.
Not for: a first visit with me, or a set done elsewhere. Book a classic full set instead.
:::

**A barber cut.**

:::copy Barber cut example
Skin fade
$40 · about 45 minutes
Includes: a skin fade on the back and sides, scissor or clipper work on top, and a neck shave.
For: short hair, whether you are keeping the same fade or changing it.
Not for: a beard. Add a beard line-up, or book the fade and beard together.
:::

Notice what the "Not for" line does in each. It names the mistake a client is most likely to make and points them at the right service, which saves you the conversation at the start of the appointment.

## Group them, and keep the groups short

Put services into a few categories in the order clients think of them: lashes, then brows, then add-ons; or cuts, then color, then treatments. The first category should be what most people come to you for.

Inside each category, put the service most people book first. Add-ons go at the end of the menu or under the service they belong to, never mixed in with full services where they look like a cheaper version of the real thing.

If you price every service from scratch, start from a price list built for your trade. The [nail tech price list](/guides/nail-tech-price-list/) shows the structure even if you are not a nail tech, and [pricing transparency](/blog/pricing-transparency-in-hair-bookings-build-client-trust/) covers why the number belongs on the menu at all.

## The honest trade-off: a long menu feels thorough and makes choosing harder

There is a pull toward listing everything. Every length, every variation, a separate line for short, medium and long hair. It looks complete, and it feels like good service.

But every extra choice is one more thing a new client has to understand before they can book. A menu of forty services makes a stranger read forty descriptions to find the three that apply to them, and the more alike they look, the more likely they pick wrong or give up and message you.

So cut where you can. Fold variations into one service with a price range and say what moves it. Drop the service you have not been booked for in six months. Keep the long list for yourself and give clients the short one. The cost is real, too: a shorter menu means some appointments arrive under a broader name and you sort out the detail in the chair. For most books that is a better trade than a client who never booked.

## Questions

### What should a salon service description include?

What is included in the appointment, how long it really takes, who it is for, and, when it helps, what it is not. Keep the price and the length beside every one.

### How long should a service description be?

Three or four short lines. Long enough to answer the questions a client would otherwise message you about, short enough to read at a glance on a phone.

### How many services should be on a salon menu?

As few as cover what you actually do. Fold variations into one service with a price range, put add-ons at the end, and drop anything nobody has booked in months. A shorter menu is easier to choose from.
