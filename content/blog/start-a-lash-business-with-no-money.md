You cannot start a lash business with no money at all. Training and a kit are not optional, and lash extensions are a service where bad work hurts people's eyes.

What you can do is start small, spend in the right order, and let clients pay for the rest. Plenty of people buy a logo, a sign and branded packaging first, then cannot afford the training that would have made the work good.

:::answer
The smallest honest start is training, a small kit, and a room you rent by the day inside an existing salon. Training comes first and is the one cost never to cut, because lash work happens millimetres from an open eye and licensing varies by state. What actually gets you booked is free: photos of your own work, a booking page, a Google Business Profile and a deposit on every booking. Branding, a sign and paid ads all wait.
:::

## Training is the first money out and the last money to cut

Lash extensions go on millimetres from an open eye, with adhesive that gives off fumes, on a client lying down with her eyes closed. Poor isolation breaks natural lashes. Adhesive on the lid burns. A client who leaves with an irritation does not leave a review, she tells people.

Look for hands-on time on a real model, watched and corrected, not only a mannequin head. Classics before volume.

Licensing you sort out yourself. Whether lash extensions sit under esthetics, cosmetology or a separate route varies by state and changes, so read your own state board rather than an Instagram caption. Get insured before your first paying client.

## A small kit you know well beats a big cheap one

The beginner instinct is every length, four curls, three adhesives and a drawer of tweezers. In practice you use a narrow band of it and never learn one adhesive well enough to troubleshoot.

- A few lengths and one or two curls that cover most of the clients you will actually see. Add a curl when someone needs one.
- One adhesive, until you know how it behaves in your room, at your speed, in summer and in winter. Adhesive trouble is nearly always humidity, cure time or technique, none of which you can diagnose while switching bottles.
- Good tweezers, the one line worth spending up on. Cheap ones lose tip alignment and you blame your own hands.
- Eye pads, lash cleanser, a light you can see isolation under, and a secondhand massage table rather than a branded lash bed.

## The cheapest room to work in is not your own

The ladder, cheapest first:

1. **A room at home, where that is allowed.** No rent. Check your lease and local rules first.
2. **A room rented by the day inside an existing salon.** This is how most people bridge the gap, because you pay only for the days you have clients. Two days a week to start, three when the book justifies it.
3. **A chair in a shared studio, monthly.** More freedom, and a cost that arrives whether or not anyone booked.
4. **Your own space, last.** The right move eventually, the wrong first move.

> Rent by the day and your rent follows your book. Sign a monthly lease and your book has to catch up to your rent.

## What gets you booked is free, and what can wait is the expensive part

**Before and after photos of your own work, on real people.** Same angle, same light, eyes closed, no filter, every single set. Your own hands, or it falls apart the first time a set does not match the picture.

**A booking page.** A link clients tap from your Instagram bio that shows real availability and takes a deposit. BKRDY's Starter plan is free, so this is not something to save up for. [The booking app comparison for solo pros](/guides/booking-app-for-solo-beauty-pros/) covers what to look for.

**A Google Business Profile.** Free, and the best reason a stranger ten minutes away finds you instead of the studio on the main road. Working from home? Set it up as a service area rather than publishing your address.

**A deposit on every booking.** A client who has paid turns up or reschedules, and both beat an empty three hour window.

What can wait: branded packaging, a logo from a designer, a sign, a second bed, paid ads. Ads are the worst of those: running them before you have photos is paying strangers to look at nothing.

## Your first clients are models, and you tell them exactly that

Charge a reduced rate and be straight about why. Not a vague "model call", which clients read as free luxury work. Say it plainly: this is practice work, the price is lower because you are still building speed, and it will take longer than it will in six months. What they do not forgive is finding out afterwards.

Then do three things with every model. **Take the photos**, both eyes, same setup. **Rebook them at the chair before they stand up**: "you are due back in about two and a half weeks, shall we put you in on the Thursday?" And **ask for a review** from every single client, including the one who went quiet at the end and the one whose set you were not happy with. Never ask only the ones who seemed pleased, never offer a discount in return, and never route unhappy ones somewhere private. Google can remove a business's reviews for any of that. [Review request templates](/guides/ask-for-reviews-templates/) has the wording.

The message to send, with your own price and timing:

:::copy Message asking friends and contacts for model clients

Hi! I have just finished my lash training and I am looking for models to build my speed and my portfolio.

Being honest about what this is: it is practice work. You get a full set of classic lashes for $45 instead of my normal price, and I need about three hours plus a few photos of your eyes closed, no face. It takes me longer than it will in six months, which is why it is cheaper now.

If anything is not right, you come back and I fix it at no charge.

Send me a day that works and I will send you my booking link.
:::

## Fills pay the bills, full sets build the book

Your first weeks are slow, and nothing is wrong. A full set takes you roughly twice as long as it will once you have speed, so a day that should hold three sets holds one and a half. That is temporary, and it is why you price off where you are heading, not off your first month.

Full sets bring new people in and fill your portfolio. Fills are shorter, repeat on a rhythm, and pay the rent, so a full set is only worth what it looks like if the client comes back. [Lash scheduling practices](/blog/lash-artist-scheduling-best-practices/) covers the day around that.

## Questions

### Do I need a license to do lash extensions?

It depends where you are. Some states regulate lash extensions under esthetics or cosmetology, others have a separate lash route, and requirements change. Check your own state board, and get insured either way.

### Can I really start without my own space?

Yes, and most people do. Renting a room by the day means you pay only for the days you have clients booked, so your overhead grows with your book.

### Should I work on models for free?

A reduced rate is usually better than free. Charging something means clients turn up and get used to paying you. Say it is practice work, why it costs less, and how long it takes.
