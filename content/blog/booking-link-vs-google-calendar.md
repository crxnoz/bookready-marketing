A free appointment link from your calendar does one job well. It shows people when you are free. If you are running on one right now you have not made a mistake. For a consultation, a patch test or a ten minute call about a color correction, it is the right tool.

The trouble starts when the thing being booked is a two hour full set with a deposit, a turnaround, and three things you need to know before they sit down. A calendar link holds none of that, so you hold it yourself, in DMs, at 9pm.

:::answer
A calendar appointment link shows your availability and stops two people taking the same slot. It cannot take a deposit or hold a card, show a priced service menu with real durations, add turnaround between clients, ask what you need to know in advance, or let a client move themselves inside a window you set. For a free consultation that is enough. For a paid appointment that costs you money when nobody turns up, the missing deposit is where the difference shows, and the fix is a booking page synced both ways with the same calendar.
:::

## What a calendar link genuinely does well

Be fair to it before replacing it. Google's appointment scheduling, and the equivalent in other calendars, reads your real calendar so it only offers time you have, refuses two people the same slot, sends an invitation, costs nothing on most accounts, and takes about ten minutes to set up.

What you get differs between a free personal account and a paid Workspace one, and that line has moved more than once, so check [Google Calendar's own help pages](https://support.google.com/calendar/) for what your account includes today.

Keep it for anything free and short: a consultation, a patch test, a call about bridal pricing. None of those has a deposit, a turnaround or an intake form.

## The deposit is the difference, and here is where it bites

Without a card at the point of booking, your no-show protection is goodwill. That is not nothing. Regulars show up. But goodwill does not cover the new client who found you on Instagram at midnight, booked the 9am Saturday, and had nothing at stake.

Picture 8:04 on a Saturday. The 9am client texts to say something came up. They booked through a calendar link, so there is no deposit to keep, no card on file, no cancellation window they agreed to, and no waitlist to offer the hour to. You are awake, you are dressed, and you cannot sell 9am at 8:04 on a Saturday.

> A calendar link loses you the Saturday morning, not the booking. The booking was never the hard part.

Some versions of calendar scheduling can attach a payment through a connected processor, and which accounts have it has changed over time. Even where it exists, it is a payment for a session, not deposit handling for a service: part now and the balance at the chair, a card held and charged only on a no-show, a refund in two taps.

## The rest of what a beauty appointment needs

- **A service menu with prices and real durations**, so a client picks a thing rather than a length of time. A calendar link asks them to choose "30 minutes" and hope.
- **Turnaround**, so the next client is not booked onto the end of a service you have not finished cleaning up from.
- **The questions you need answered before they arrive.** Allergies. What they had done last time and where. Fill or full set. Whether they are coming to you or you are driving to them.
- **A reminder they will actually see.** A calendar invitation only reminds a client who added it to a calendar they open, and plenty never accept it at all.
- **Cancel and reschedule they can do themselves**, inside a window you set. This is what removes the back and forth. Give them a button that works until 24 hours out and most of your DMs stop.
- **A page that looks like your business.** Your booking link is often the first impression after Instagram, and a generic scheduling page says something you did not mean to say. If it lives in your bio, [how you present it](/guides/add-booking-link-to-instagram-bio/) matters too.
- **A client record.** What they had, when, which color or length, what they said about it. A calendar event is a title and a time.

## Ask what you need to know before they arrive

Do this part this week whatever you book with. On a calendar link, send these the moment someone books. On a booking page they become fields the client fills in before the slot is confirmed.

:::copy Booking questions for a new client
First and last name

Phone number

Email

Which service are you booking?

First time with me, or have you been before?

If you have had this done elsewhere, when was it last done?

Any allergies or sensitivities I should know about? Adhesive, latex, acrylic, acetone, gel, fragrance, or anything you have reacted to before.

Fill or full set? If a fill, how long since your last appointment?

Coming to the studio, or do you need me to come to you? If mobile, the address and whether there is parking.

Anything about the last time you had this done that you want done differently?

I have read the deposit and cancellation policy and I agree to it.
:::

Keep the answers where only you can see them. Health answers belong in their client record, not in a calendar event title.

## Keep the calendar, and insist the sync runs both ways

The best setup for most people is not one or the other. It is a booking page clients use, connected to the personal calendar you already live in.

The direction is what matters. One-way sync pushes new bookings into your calendar, which looks fine until your own life double books you: the dentist at 2pm on Thursday sits in your calendar, your booking page never hears about it, and someone takes 2pm on Thursday.

So the question to ask a platform is not "do you sync with Google Calendar". It is "does a busy event in my own calendar block a bookable slot on my page". BKRDY syncs both directions and lets you write your own booking questions. Other platforms do it too. Ask before you pay anyone.

## For three appointments a week, a careful hand is enough

A booking page is one more thing to set up and, beyond a free tier, one more subscription. Those are real costs and nobody should talk you past them.

If you take three appointments a week, your clients are people you know, and you have not lost a Saturday to a no-show in months, a calendar link is the right answer and a booking page is an hour spent on a problem you do not have. Free tiers exist, [ours included](/pricing/), and are worth a look first. The honest threshold is the one you can feel: the week the DMs about moving appointments start eating your evenings, or the no-show that costs a number that makes you wince.

## Questions

### Can I take a deposit with a Google Calendar appointment link?

Not as a deposit against a service in the way a beauty appointment needs. Some versions can attach a payment through a connected processor, and which accounts have that has changed over time, so check Google's help pages for your own. What none of it does is take part of the price now and the balance at the chair, or hold a card and charge it only on a no-show.

### Will a booking page double book me against my own calendar?

Only if the sync runs one way. One-way sync never reads back, so a personal appointment you add yourself stays invisible to the page and someone can book over it. Two-way sync treats a busy event in your own calendar as unavailable time. Ask specifically before you sign up.

### Do I have to stop using Google Calendar if I get a booking page?

No, and you probably should not. The arrangement that works for most owners is a booking page for clients plus two-way sync with the calendar they already check, so the day stays in one place and the page stops offering time your own life has taken.
