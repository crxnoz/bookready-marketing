If your book is full weeks ahead and you are still turning people away, your price is the problem, not your calendar. You have run out of hours to sell, so the only way to earn more is to earn more for each one.

That is the whole case. The hard part is the message, the regulars, and the week after you send it, and those are what this is about.

:::answer
Raise your prices when your book is full or nearly full most weeks and you are turning people away. Pick an increase you can say in one line, give two to four weeks' notice with a date, keep already booked appointments at the old price, and decide in advance what your longest-standing regulars get. Expect a few clients to leave. If your book is thin, do not raise yet.
:::

## Why January is the easy moment

A new year is when clients expect prices to move, so a change in January reads as routine rather than personal. And December is usually your busiest month, which means most of your regulars are in your chair in the weeks before it, where you can tell them face to face.

So the timing that works: decide in early December, tell people at their December appointments and in one message, and start the new prices in the first or second week of January. Your December bookings keep the old price, which takes the sting out of it.

## How to tell it is time

Look at the last two months, not the last two days. It is time when most of these are true:

- You are booked a week or two ahead, most weeks, not only in December.
- You turn people away for the same days and times again and again.
- New clients wait longer to get in than your regulars wait between visits.
- Your costs have gone up since you last set prices, and your prices have not.
- You cannot remember the last time you raised them.

If only one is true, wait. If your book is thin, wait longer. Raising into empty slots usually makes them emptier, and the work there is getting the right clients in. The [daily capacity sum](/blog/how-many-clients-per-day/) shows whether you are really full or just tired.

## How much

Work it out from what you can afford to lose, not from a percent someone else uses. Here is a worked example with round numbers.

Say you see 30 clients a week at $60. That is $1,800. Raise to $70 and 26 clients bring in $1,820. So you could lose 4 of your 30 and still earn the same, with four hours back.

Run your own version of that sum before you pick the number. It turns the fear of losing clients into a figure: how many could go before the raise costs you anything.

Keep the increase easy to say. A new round number, $60 to $70, reads as a decision. $60 to $63.50 reads as an apology. And raise by service, not as one flat percent across everything: the service you are most booked for, and the one that takes longest, have the most room.

This is the same answer to the wider question of how to charge for your services, whether you work in a salon or price your services as a freelancer: the price should follow demand for your time, and your time is the thing you cannot make more of.

## New clients first, or everyone at once

There are two ways to do it, and both work.

**New clients first.** New bookings pay the new price, existing clients keep the old one for now. It is a quiet test: if new clients still book at $70, you know the price holds. The cost is two price lists, and regulars who notice they are on a different one.

**Everyone at once.** One date, one price list, one message. Simpler to run and nothing to keep track of. The cost is that every client meets the change on the same day.

If you have never raised before and you are nervous, start with new clients and move everyone over within a few months. If you have done it before, do everyone at once.

## How much notice

Two to four weeks is enough for most services. Give a date, not "soon". Send it once, put it on your booking page, and mention it at the appointment before it takes effect.

Anything already booked keeps the old price. Changing the price of an appointment someone has already made feels like a trick, even when you meant nothing by it.

## What to do with long-time regulars

The client who has come every three weeks for five years is the one you will dread telling. You have three fair options.

- **The same increase, with longer notice.** Tell them first, a month or two ahead, in person. Most of them will understand, and some will have expected it.
- **A grace period.** They keep the old price until a set date, say three months away, then move to the new one.
- **A smaller step.** They go up less this time and catch up at the next increase.

What not to do is promise a permanent old price. In a year or two your most loyal clients are sitting in your best slots at your lowest rate, and you are raising everyone else to make up for it.

## The message

Short, warm, and without an apology. Say what changes, when, and that their booked appointments are safe. Do not explain your rent.

:::copy Price increase notice
Hi Renee, a quick note: from Monday, January 11, my prices are changing. A gel set will be $70 and a fill $55. Anything you have already booked stays at the current price. Thank you for being a regular. It means a lot. My booking page has the full new list.
:::

Send it to everyone affected on the same day, so nobody hears it secondhand. Change your price list and booking page on the date you said, not before and not a week later.

When someone replies upset, answer once, kindly, with the same facts. Do not negotiate in the thread. If you want to make an exception, make it in person, on purpose.

## The trade-off

Some clients will leave. That is not the plan failing. It is part of the point.

The ones most likely to go are the ones who chose you for the price, and the hours they free up go to people who wanted you for the work. In the worked example, losing four clients cost nothing and gave four hours back. That is room for a longer service, a new client, or a day that ends at six.

But the trade only holds when demand is real. If you raise and your book empties for months, you raised too far or too early. Hold the new price for a full rebooking cycle before you judge it, then decide.

This week, pull up the last two months of bookings, count how often you said no, and run the sum on your most booked service.

## Questions

### How much should I raise my prices?

Work out how many clients you could lose and still earn the same. In the worked example, going from $60 to $70 means 26 clients earn what 30 did before. Pick a round number you can say in one line, and raise the services you are most booked for first.

### How much notice should I give before raising prices?

Two to four weeks, with a set date, and longer for your longest-standing regulars. Keep anything already booked at the old price.

### Should I raise prices for existing clients or only new ones?

Either works. New clients first is a quiet test of the new price but leaves you with two lists for a while. Everyone at once is simpler. If you have never raised before, starting with new clients and moving everyone across within a few months is the gentler route.
