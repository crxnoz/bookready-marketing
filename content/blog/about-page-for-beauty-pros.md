Nobody reads an about section for your life story. A new client opens it with three questions, usually without putting them into words: who is actually going to do my hair, are they good at the thing I want, and will I feel comfortable sitting in that chair for two hours.

Answer those and you are done. Most about sections answer none of them, because they open with a childhood love of hair, list every course since 2014, and end on a mission statement about empowering everyone to feel beautiful. A reader skims it, learns nothing they needed, and goes back to the photos.

:::answer
A good about section is four or five plain sentences: who you are and what you do, what you are best at, who you are a good fit for, what a visit with you is like, and where and when to find you. Put a real photo of you beside it. Cut the origin story, the course list, the mission statement and any word you would not say out loud to a client.
:::

## What a new client is checking

Read your about section as a stranger deciding between you and two others nearby.

**Who will do my hair.** A name and a face. If you are a team, who the client will see, or how they choose. A "we" with no names leaves them guessing who they will get.

**Are they good at what I want.** Not good in general. Good at blonding, at curly cuts, at volume lashes, at brow lamination. A client with a specific want is looking for that word. If it is not there, they assume it is not your thing.

**Will I feel comfortable.** This is the one that decides it more often than the others. Is it quiet or social, rushed or unhurried, a big salon or a room at the back of a house? Is the person going to judge me for the box dye or for the gap since my last appointment? One sentence about what a visit is like answers more of this than a paragraph of adjectives.

## The five sentences

Each one has a job. Write them in this order.

1. **Who you are and what you do.** Your name, your trade, where. "I am Rosa, a colorist working from a private suite in East Austin."
2. **What you are best at.** The thing you want more of. Name it plainly. "Most of my clients come to me for lived-in blondes and for correcting color that went wrong somewhere else."
3. **Who you are a good fit for.** Including, if it helps, who you are not. "If you want low-maintenance color that grows out softly, we will get on. If you want platinum every four weeks, I will point you to someone better suited."
4. **What a visit is like.** The feel of the room and the pace. "It is one chair, one client at a time, and I block enough time that nobody is rushed."
5. **Where and when.** Briefly, because the rest of your site has the details. "I work Tuesday to Saturday. Parking is on the street."

That is it. Read it out loud. If any sentence sounds like something you would not say to a client across the chair, rewrite it until it does.

## The template

:::copy About section template
I am [your name], a [your trade] working from [where: your suite, studio, salon, or home studio] in [neighborhood or city].
Most of my clients come to me for [the one or two services you are best at].
I am a good fit if you want [what your best clients want]. [Optional: If you want (something you do not do well), I am happy to recommend someone.]
[What a visit is like: the room, the pace, one client at a time or a busy salon floor.]
I work [days], and [one practical detail: parking, entrance, how to find you].
:::

For a team, keep the same five sentences for the salon as a whole, then give each person one or two lines of their own: their name, what they are best at, and the days they work.

## What to cut

- **The origin story.** "I have loved doing hair since I was six" is true for nearly every stylist, which is why it tells a new client nothing.
- **The full course list.** One line on training is fine if it matters to the client, for example a specialist certification in the service they are looking for. A list of every class reads like a resume nobody asked for.
- **The mission statement.** "Empowering every client to feel confident" is a sentence no client has ever searched for.
- **Stacked adjectives.** Luxurious, welcoming, passionate, dedicated. Show it in the sentence about what a visit is like instead.

## The photo matters as much as the words

Put a recent photo of you next to it, at work if possible, where your face is clear. Not a logo, not a product shot, not a picture of a client. The question "who is going to do my hair?" is answered by a face faster than by any sentence.

If you would rather not show your face, a photo of your hands at work or your actual room is the next best thing.

## The honest trade-off

A specific about section turns some people away. Saying you are best at lived-in blondes means the client who wants a sharp platinum might keep scrolling. That is the point: it sends the clients you want toward the Book button and the others toward someone better for them. But if your book is thin and you need every booking you can get right now, you may want to write sentence three more broadly for a while.

And an about section cannot rescue a gallery that does not show the work. If a client does not like what they see in your photos, no paragraph will change their mind. Write the five sentences, but spend just as much time on the [gallery](/platform/).

## Where it goes

Most booking websites have a place for this. On a BKRDY site it is the About section, which you edit from the Website page alongside the rest of your site. Wherever it lives, keep it on the same page as your services and the booking button, so the client never has to leave to decide.

## Questions

### What should I write in my about me as a hairstylist?

Five sentences: who you are and where you work, what you are best at, who you are a good fit for, what a visit with you is like, and when and where to find you. Next to it, a recent photo of you at work.

### How long should a salon about page be?

Four or five sentences for a solo pro. For a team, the same five for the salon, plus one or two lines for each person with their name, what they are best at and the days they work.

### Should I list my certifications on my about page?

Only the ones a client would care about, usually a specialist certification in the service they came looking for. One line is enough. A full list of courses reads like a resume and hides the sentences that matter.
