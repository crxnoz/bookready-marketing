How far ahead should you let clients book?

October 27, 2026 · 6 min read

Four to six weeks is the right window for most beauty work, and most owners have their books open far longer than that. Three months, six months, or rolling open forever, because a full calendar feels like a safe calendar.

A booking made eight weeks out is a guess. A booking made ten days out is a plan. When a client picks a Tuesday in December halfway through October, they do not yet know about the work trip that will land on it. The appointment is real on your calendar and provisional in their head, and that gap is where most late cancellations come from.

The short answer

For most beauty work, open your books four to six weeks ahead, not three or six months. A rough rule: about one and a half times the gap between a client's visits, so a lash artist doing fills every three weeks opens four to five weeks, and a colorist seeing clients every eight weeks opens ten to twelve. Keep a genuinely long window only for bridal, large color corrections, and anything a client plans travel around.

The further ahead a slot is booked, the more likely it moves

A long window does not create demand. It lets the demand you already have land earlier, in less reliable form. The client who would have booked the 20th books the 20th of next month instead, and in between, their life rearranges around a slot they can no longer make.

A long booking window does not get you more clients. It gets you the same clients, committed earlier and less certainly.

Here is the number to run this week. Pull every cancellation and no-show from the last three months and write next to each one how many days passed between the booking and the appointment. If they cluster at the far end, your real window is shorter than the one you are running.

Three things decide your window

How often your clients come back. The big one. Cover roughly one and a half visit cycles. Shorter than one cycle and regulars genuinely cannot book. Longer than two and you are collecting guesses.

Whether you take a deposit. A client who paid $40 in October moves their appointment rather than vanishing, so deposits buy you a longer window. If you take nothing up front, keep it tight.

How seasonal your work is. If December and prom season are the only times your book fills eight weeks out, you need a longer window for two months of the year, not permanently.

Starting windows, by the kind of work you do

Starting points, not rules. Set one, run it two months, then move it based on what your own cancellations say.

If you offer several of these, set the window for your most frequent service and take the long tail by request.

A shorter window plus a waitlist usually beats a long one

The fear with shortening is the client who wants to book and cannot. A waitlist answers that better than an open calendar does, because it turns a cancellation into a filled slot instead of a hole.

The client who wanted next Thursday says so, and when Thursday's 2pm cancels at 9am that client gets the offer and takes it. Without a waitlist you are refreshing your own calendar and posting the opening to a story half your clients will not see for six hours. The window and the waitlist are two halves of the same fix, and reducing no-shows covers the rest.

Some work genuinely needs a long window

Do not shorten your way into a problem. Three kinds of booking need real distance:

  • Bridal. The date was set before you were. The window has to reach it.
  • Large color corrections. Multiple sessions planned as a sequence, often timed to an event.
  • Anything a client books travel around. They buy the flight after they have the appointment.

The move is not to open your whole calendar for these. Handle them by request, off the public window, so one bridal party does not force every regular to scroll to next March.

Let your regulars rebook at the chair

The clients who like booking far ahead are almost always your best ones, and they are the ones you need no public window for. You are standing next to them. Book the next appointment before they leave, into a date the public calendar has not opened yet.

This is what makes a shorter window painless. Regulars get first pick of every date weeks before anyone else sees it, which is a real perk that costs nothing. If rebooking at the chair is not a habit yet, how to rebook clients is where to start, because this approach rests on it.

Open dates in batches, on a day your clients learn

Instead of a calendar that rolls open one day at a time forever, open a defined block on a set day. The first Monday of the month, four weeks of dates, every month. Clients learn the rhythm quickly and the regulars start showing up for it. You get a predictable moment to look at your month before it fills, instead of finding out in week three that you gave away every Saturday.

The honest cost: it takes explaining, and a few clients will message you confused the first month. Put the note below on your booking page and in your confirmation email. And it is not worth doing at all if you take a handful of bookings a week.

The note for your booking page and clients

New dates open on the first Monday of every month at 9am, and cover the next four weeks. If the date you want is not showing yet, it is not taken, it just has not opened. Check back on the first Monday, or join the waitlist on my booking page and you will get an email if something comes free. Already a regular? Book your next appointment before you leave and I will get you in ahead of the public release.

Set the window once, run it two months, and let your own cancellation list decide whether to move it. You already have that list.

Questions

Will a shorter booking window cost me bookings?

Usually not. The demand does not disappear when the slot does, it moves to the next available date or onto your waitlist. What you lose is the booking the client was never really sure about.

What do I say to a client who wants to book six months out?

Tell them you open dates a month at a time so nobody holds a slot they are not sure of, then offer something concrete. Their next appointment at the chair today, or the waitlist for the week they want.

Which day should I release new dates on?

Pick the quietest morning of your week and keep it there forever. The day matters far less than never changing it, because the whole value of a batch release is that clients can predict it.
