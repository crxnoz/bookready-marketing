Buffer time between appointments: the quiet fix

November 2, 2026 · 6 min read

If your day is thirty minutes late by 3pm, the cause is almost never the one client who talked too much. It is the calendar. Your service times are set to the best possible version of each service: the client who arrives early, sits still, pays on the first tap and leaves.

Turnaround. The client who arrives six minutes late. The one who wants to talk at the end. The card that will not read. The sweep up. None of that is in the calendar, so it comes out of the next person's appointment.

The short answer

Buffer time between appointments is the gap you leave for what happens around a service rather than during it: turnaround, a late arrival, the chat at the end, taking payment. Find yours by noting when each appointment actually ended for one week and comparing that to when it was due to end. For most solo pros the best place for that gap is inside the service length itself, because a padded block travels with the appointment when you move it by hand.

A day runs late for structural reasons, not personal ones

A 45 minute fill really is 45 minutes of lashing. That number is honest. But the appointment is not the service. It starts when the last client stands up and ends when the next one is in the chair and ready, and nobody books that version.

So lateness compounds instead of canceling out. By mid afternoon you are not recovering, you are getting further behind while apologizing more.

One slow client costs you once. A calendar with no gaps charges you on every appointment for the rest of the day.

Find your real service time before you set a number

For one week, write down when each appointment actually ended. Not when it was due to end. Phone notes, the back of a card, whatever you will actually do while your hands are busy.

At the end of the week, put the two columns side by side: booked end, real end. The difference is your buffer, and it is usually more consistent than you expect. A full set that runs twelve minutes over runs twelve minutes over most weeks. A 30 minute brow wax might run four.

  • Measure per service, not per day. Long and short services leak different amounts, so you will end up with two or three numbers. Fills against full sets are the clearest case, and part of what makes lash scheduling its own puzzle.
  • Use the number that shows up most often, not your worst Saturday. Pad for the worst case and every ordinary day pays for one that happens once a month.

Pad the service, or set a separate buffer

Two ways to build it in, and they are not the same decision.

For most solo pros, pad the service length. One number instead of two, an honest expectation for the client, and a gap that survives the day you book someone in by hand at 4:15, which is the first thing a separate buffer quietly stops doing.

Keep it separate when the time matters outside your own day: you price strictly per hour, or the gap belongs to a room rather than to you.

Buffer does cost you slots, and it is still worth it

Count the appointments you fit into a full day now. Add your buffer to each and count again. On a day booked open to close, the difference is a real appointment you will not take and real money you will not make. Anyone who says buffer is free is selling you something.

Set that against what running late costs, which never arrives as a line item. The last client of the day, who waited and will remember waiting. Their review. The appointment they would have rebooked on the way out. And it only bites on fully booked days, the ones you needed to go well.

So run it on your own book. How many days last month were full from open to close? If it is most of them, buffer costs you real income, and the answer is to price for the longer service. If it is a handful, it costs almost nothing and buys back every one of those days.

Buffer works better when the client knows the window

Buffer absorbs a six minute late arrival. It does not absorb twenty. Say what the appointment time means once on your booking page, next to whatever you already say about deposits and no-shows.

A line for your booking page

Please arrive right on time, or a few minutes early if you can. Your appointment is held just for you and the time is built around your service, so a late start turns into a rushed one. If you are running more than 15 minutes behind, text me and I will tell you honestly whether we can still do the full service or whether it is kinder to move you. Either way you are not in trouble. I would rather do your service properly than quickly.

The one place buffer does not belong

Back to back short services with no setup. A run of 15 minute brow waxes, beard trims, a polish change, a quick removal. Nothing to strip down, nothing to sanitize beyond a wipe.

Pad each of those by ten minutes and you have thrown away most of an afternoon, and you will not spend those gaps resting. You will spend them on your phone. Keep them tight and take one real gap in the middle of the run instead. Fifteen minutes after four quick clients does more for you than four four minute gaps that each evaporate.

The end of day buffer that almost nobody sets

Leave the last thirty minutes of your day unbookable. Not a break, just not available.

If the day ran over, it runs into those thirty minutes rather than into your evening. If it ran on time, you get thirty minutes to clean, restock or leave early.

The last slot is also the one most likely to run long, because it usually goes to someone coming straight from work. A hard close is what makes those thirty minutes real, because otherwise you will fill them the first time someone asks nicely. Questions

How much buffer time should I leave between appointments?

Whatever your own week says, usually five to fifteen minutes for most beauty services. Do not borrow a number. Note your real end times for one week, compare them to the booked ones, and use the gap that shows up most often.

Should the buffer sit inside the service or separate from it?

Inside the service length for most solo pros: one number, an honest expectation, and a gap that moves with the appointment when you reschedule by hand. Keep it separate when you price strictly by the hour, or when the gap belongs to a shared room.

Does buffer time cost me money?

On a fully booked day, yes. It removes slots you could have sold. The question is whether those slots are worth more than the rushed services, the waiting last client and the rebookings you lose.
