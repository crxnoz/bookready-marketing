How to start a nail business at home

November 12, 2026 · 5 min read

Two things decide whether this is possible, and you can check both before buying a lamp. Nail services are licensed by your state board, and what you need varies by state. Whether you may see clients at home at all can come down to your lease or your building.

Confirm the first with your state board, the second with your landlord or local authority. Then set up the room.

The short answer

Starting a nail business at home means confirming your state board's licensing rules and whether your lease or building allows clients, then setting up one room with real ventilation, light you can judge color under, and a table and chair you can both sit at for two hours. After that it is a short menu, prices set by your market and your speed rather than your address, your own photos, one booking link, and a deposit on every appointment.

Ventilation first, then light, then the furniture

Ventilation is the thing people cut, because it is the only item here that does not photograph well. Do not cut it. Filing dust and product vapor need somewhere to go that is not the rest of your house.

What works: a window that opens in the room you work in, a fan placed to move air out rather than stir it around, and a source-capture extractor at the table if you use a liquid system.

Then light. You cannot match color under a warm ceiling bulb. One lamp on an arm, bright and neutral, set so your hands do not shadow the nails.

Then the furniture, where your body is made or broken:

  • A table at a height that suits you both, so nobody reaches up or hunches down.
  • A chair with real back support. You sit in yours for six hours, the client two.
  • A surface you can clean properly: hard, non-porous, no fabric, no untreated wood.

Clients in your home means your home has opening hours

A salon has a door, a reception, and a closing time whether you enforce it or not. At home, you are the door. Decide five things first.

Arrival instruction. Say where to park or which buzzer to press, and to come at the appointment time, not before.

A finishing time you hold. "My last appointment starts at 6pm" is a sentence you can say kindly and repeat forever.

Somewhere to wait that is not your kitchen. One chair in the hall and a glass of water.

Pets. Say in advance if there is a dog or a cat in the house. Some people are allergic, some are frightened, and both need to know beforehand.

The person who arrives forty minutes early. Have the line ready: "I'm with a client until 2, there's a coffee place on the corner, text me when you're back."

The note you send the day before a first home appointment

Hi Mara, looking forward to seeing you tomorrow at 2pm for your gel fill. I'm at 14 Ashcroft Road, the blue door on the left. Buzz 3B and I'll let you in. Street parking on that block is usually easy after 10am. Come at 2pm rather than early if you can, I'll be finishing with someone right before you. There's a chair in the hall if you do. One heads up: I have a small dog, Pepa. She stays in the back room during appointments, so tell me if you would rather not see her. See you tomorrow.

Buy for the menu you have, not the one you might have

Write the menu first: three or four things you are good at today, then buy for those only.

  • Sanitation: a disinfectant meant for salon implements, used for the full contact time on its label, and enough sets to finish a day without rushing a cycle.
  • Implements: nippers, pushers, files and buffers in the grits your services use.
  • Products for your menu only: base, your builder or acrylic system, top coat, and a color range you can finish a set with.
  • A curing lamp matched to that system, towels, table barriers, a lidded bin.

What waits: a second color line, a chrome collection, a drill if you are not doing art.

Working from home is not a reason to charge less

Starting low is the hardest thing in this business to undo: the clients who came for the price are the first to leave when you raise it. Two things set your prices, and your address is not one of them: what the work is worth in your market, and how fast you can do it well.

Your client is paying for the work. The address is not the product.

Look at what people near you charge for the same service at the same quality, and place yourself honestly in that range. If you are slower than most, price what the service really takes you. A price list built properly is worth an evening.

The first clients come from your own photos and one link

Your own photos of your own work. Not reposts, not supplier images. Same corner of the table, same light, hands at the same angle, shot after the cleanup.

A booking page you can send as one link. One URL with your services, your times and your deposit, that takes the booking without a back and forth. Put it in your Instagram bio and send it to every "how much" message. If you are weighing up tools, start here.

A Google Business Profile. People search for a nail tech near them and tap the map. Google's rules on showing an address versus a service area depend on whether clients come to you, so read the current guidance.

Reviews, asked for properly. Ask every client, not only the ones you can tell are pleased. Never offer a discount or anything else in return, and never ask people to rate you privately first and send only the happy ones on to Google. Those shortcuts can get a business's reviews removed.

Take a deposit from the very first booking

In a salon, an 11am no-show gets filled by a walk-in or a colleague takes the chair. At home it is a dead hour in your own hallway, with no footfall to fill it from. That is why the deposit goes in from day one.

A flat amount or a percentage of the service both work, taken at booking and applied to the total. What matters is that it is automatic and applies to everybody, friends included. How much to charge is its own decision. BKRDY's Free plan costs nothing, so cost is no reason for the booking page to wait.

What you save in rent, you pay for somewhere else

You save the rent, the split or the booth fee, and you keep your own hours. You give up walk-ins and footfall, colleagues who cover you when your kid is sick and tell you when your shaping has gone lazy, and the line between work and the rest of your life. The table is in the next room, so the work follows you to the sofa.

Some people get their best years out of it. Others find within a year that they want a room somewhere else, and that is information, not failure. Keep the client list, the photos and the booking link in your own name, so the day you take a suite, the business walks in with you.
