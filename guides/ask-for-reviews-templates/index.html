Guides / Getting them back

Copy-paste templates to ask clients for reviews. Text, email, in person.

August 9, 2026 · 11 min read

You already know reviews matter. The problem is not motivation, it is the wording. You finish a set of nails you are genuinely proud of, the client says something lovely on her way out, and then the moment passes because you did not have the words ready. Or you do send something, and it comes out stiff and corporate: "We value your feedback. Please take a moment to rate your experience." Nobody answers that.

The fix is small and boring. Have the exact words ready to paste. That is the whole guide. Below you get a working toolkit: four short text messages for the post-visit window, two warmer emails with subject lines, and a spoken script for the chair and the front desk. Every one uses [BRACKETED] fields so you swap in the client and the service and it still reads like you wrote it.

You also get the parts most template lists skip: the timing that makes or breaks the ask, a follow-up cadence that nudges once and then stops, and the rule about who never gets a request. These work on any platform you use today. At the end, we show how BKRDY's reviews feature emails a verified request on its own after a visit so you can stop copy-pasting. If you run your own book, start with the nail tech setup.

Five rules every review request follows.

Before the templates, the five rules that make them work. Skip these and the best wording in the world underperforms.

1. Time it to the glow. Ask within a couple of hours of a great service, while the client is still admiring the work. Enthusiasm has a short shelf life. A week later, the same client likes you just as much but cannot be bothered.

2. Make it one tap. Paste the direct review link. Never write "search for us on Google." Every extra step loses people, and asking a client to go hunting for your listing is three steps too many.

3. Keep it short. One or two sentences. A paragraph reads like a form letter and gets skimmed. The message has one job: get a tap on the link.

4. Personalize it. Use their name and the actual service. "Thanks for coming in" is a blast. "Loved doing that chrome set today, Maya" is a message from a person.

5. Ask once, nudge once, stop. Two touches total. The nudge catches the people who meant to and forgot. A third makes you the person who will not let it go. For the strategy behind all of this, read how to get more salon reviews.

Text-message templates (the workhorses).

Text is where most reviews come from. It gets opened in minutes and it fits the two-sentence rule naturally. Swap the bracketed fields and send. These four cover almost every situation you will hit in a normal week.

1. Same-day post-visit ask. Hi [Client first name], so glad you came in today. If you have 30 seconds, a quick review really helps other people find me: [Review link]. Thank you.

2. Warmer version that names the service. [Client first name], I loved doing your [Service] today, that color came out exactly right. If you are happy with it, would you mind leaving a quick review? It makes a real difference for a small studio: [Review link]

3. For a long-time regular. Hi [Client first name], you have been coming to me for [length of time] now and I appreciate it more than you know. Would you leave a short review sometime this week? Your word carries a lot of weight: [Review link]

4. The one gentle follow-up. Hey [Client first name], no pressure at all, just floating this back up in case it got buried: [Review link]. Thanks either way, see you next time.

Send these from your own phone. BKRDY's review requests go by email, not text, and you can see how the messaging side fits together on the notifications page.

Email templates for a warmer, longer ask.

Email gives you room to be gracious. It suits clients who do not text, and it is the right format for a batched monthly ask. Same discipline applies: one call to action, one link.

1. Post-visit thank you plus ask. Subject: Thank you for coming in, [Client first name]. Body: Hi [Client first name], thank you for trusting me with your [Service] today. Sitting in a chair for two hours with someone is a real vote of confidence and I do not take it for granted. If you were happy with how it turned out, would you leave a short review? A few honest sentences help people decide whether [Salon name] is right for them, and they help me more than any ad I could run. [Review link]. Thanks again, [Stylist/Tech name].

2. Monthly ask to happy regulars. Subject: A small favor, [Client first name]. Body: Hi [Client first name], you have been in a few times now and I wanted to say thank you properly. If you have a minute this week, a review would mean a lot. I am a small studio, so a handful of recent reviews genuinely changes who walks through the door. Here is the link: [Review link]. No pressure, and see you at your next appointment. [Stylist/Tech name].

You do not have to send these by hand forever. On paid plans, review requests by email can run on their own through the reviews tool.

The in-person script (say this at checkout).

The spoken ask converts better than anything you can type, and most owners never make it because it feels like begging. It is not. You did good work and you are asking the person who just enjoyed it to say so.

The core line. "If you loved it, a quick review really helps people find me. I will text you the link right now before you go."

That last clause is the whole trick. You are not asking them to remember anything. You send the link while they are standing at the counter putting their card away, and the notification is on their phone before they reach the car.

Natural variations. "Would you do me a favor? If you are happy with it, leave me a review. I will send the link now." Or, for a regular: "You have been with me a while now, would you mind writing a couple of lines about it? Sending the link over."

Follow through immediately. Send it while they are still in front of you. An ask with no link attached is a nice conversation that produces nothing.

Train the team. If someone else runs checkout, make the line part of the closing routine, right after payment and rebooking. Say it every time and it stops feeling like a favor and starts feeling like the last step. More on the wider strategy in the salon reviews guide.

Exactly when to send each message.

Same words, different hour, very different result. Here is the timing per channel.

In person. At checkout, every happy client, no exceptions. This is the highest converting moment you will ever get because you are looking at each other.

Text. Same day, one to two hours after the appointment ends. They have shown someone at work, taken a photo, gotten a compliment. The feeling is at its peak and your message lands right on top of it.

Email. That evening or the next morning for clients who live in their inbox. For regulars, a batched ask once a month is plenty.

Why same-day beats next-week. Enthusiasm decays. On the day of, the service is a specific memory with details attached. A week later it is a fact about their calendar. People write generous, specific reviews when the detail is still there, and the reviews that mention a specific service are the ones that convince strangers.

The rule that overrides all of it. Never send a request to a client who seemed unhappy, rushed, or quiet at the end. Reach out privately and fix it first. If something has already gone public, here is how to respond to a negative review.

Follow-up cadence: how to nudge without nagging.

Two touches. That is the entire cadence, and the restraint is the point.

Day 0. The first ask, in person at checkout, followed by the text with the link while they are still there. If you only ever do this one thing, you will still see your review count climb.

Day 3 or 4. One gentle follow-up if no review has appeared. Use template 4 from the text section. Keep it light and give them an out.

Then stop. No third message. Ever.

The reasoning is simple. Most people who do not respond to the first ask are not refusing, they got distracted between the parking lot and dinner. A single nudge recovers a good share of them. Past that, you are no longer reminding, you are pressuring someone who has already made a decision, and you are trading a client relationship worth hundreds a year for one review.

Know who already left one. Nothing undercuts a thank you faster than asking a client for a review they wrote last week. Keep a note on the client record so you can skip them next time. Your client list is the right place for it, and it stops the awkward double ask before it happens.

Using merge fields so every message reads personal.

Every template above uses the same five fields. Fill them properly and a templated message stops sounding templated.

[Client first name]. First name only, and spelled the way they spell it. Full names read like a bill.

[Stylist/Tech name]. Who did the work. In a multi-chair studio this matters, because the client's loyalty is usually to a person, not a business.

[Service]. The specific one. Not "your appointment" but "your builder gel fill" or "that chrome set." This single field does more work than the other four combined.

[Review link]. The direct link to wherever you want the review, usually your Google Business profile. Shorten it if it looks ugly in a text.

[Salon name]. Mostly for email, where it grounds the message.

The gap between a 2 percent and a 15 percent response rate is often nothing more than the name and the service. The catch is that filling five fields by hand for every client is exactly the chore that makes you stop sending. Questions

What should a review request message say?

Thank the client by name, name the exact service they had, and give them a direct one-tap link. Two sentences is plenty. Ask only when the visit went well, and make the link the last thing in the message so there is nothing to scroll past.

Is it better to ask for reviews by text or email?

Text wins for the same-day ask because it gets opened within minutes. Email suits a warmer, longer message or a batched ask to a segment of regulars. Most studios use both: text for the post-visit window, email for the monthly sweep.

How many times should I follow up?

Once. Ask on day zero, send one short nudge around day three or four, then stop. The single nudge recovers the clients who simply forgot. A third message reads as pestering and costs you more goodwill than the review is worth.

When should I send a review request?

Same day, ideally within one to two hours of the appointment ending, while the client is still looking at their nails or their hair in the car mirror. For email, that evening or the next morning still works well. Conversion drops fast after 48 hours.

How do I personalize a review request at scale?

Use merge fields for the client first name, the stylist or tech, and the specific service. BKRDY's own request fills in the client's first name and the service, so it still reads one to one without you typing anything.

Yes, on Solo, Studio and Studio+. After an appointment is over, BKRDY emails a verified review request with the client name and service already filled in, at the delay you choose. It asks each client at most once every 90 days.

Can I text clients for reviews from BKRDY?

No. BKRDY sends review requests by email only. To ask by text, send the templates from your own phone in the post-visit window and let the automated email requests run in the background. The wording in this guide works for both.

Should I ask every client for a review?

Ask every happy client, every visit. Never send a request to someone who seemed unhappy or rushed out quietly. Resolve that privately first. A review request to an unhappy client is an invitation to write the complaint in public.
