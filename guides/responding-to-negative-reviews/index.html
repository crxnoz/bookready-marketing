Home / Guides / How to respond to a negative salon review

Guide · 9 min read

How to respond to a negative salon review. Acknowledge, take it offline, and never argue in public.

A one-star review just landed, and you can feel the heat rising. The instinct is universal: defend yourself, explain what really happened, and prove the client wrong right there in the open where everyone can see you were right. Resist it. That instinct is exactly the wrong move, because the audience for your reply is almost never the person who wrote it. Future clients are reading your response, not the complaint, and a defensive reply does more damage to your bookings than the original one-star ever could. Here is the BookReady position: a bad review is not a verdict, it is an audition in front of every prospect who will ever read your profile. Handled well, a calm reply turns a one-star into a quiet win and makes your whole page read as run by a real, fair human who owns their mistakes. Most salons are one steady voice away from turning their worst review into their most persuasive one. This guide gives you the whole playbook: a simple response framework you can use every time (acknowledge, take it offline, never argue publicly), the moves that quietly torch your reputation even when you are in the right, how to turn a one-star into a rebooking, and the narrow cases where you should report and dispute a fake or defamatory review instead of replying to it. Calm beats clever here, every single time.

First

Before you type anything: wait an hour.

The single most damaging review responses are written in the first ten minutes, while the owner is still angry and the reply feels justified. It never reads that way to a stranger. So before you touch the keyboard, do one thing: read the review once, close the tab, and come back in an hour. Nothing about a bad review gets worse in sixty minutes, and almost everything about your reply gets better. That pause is not weakness, it is strategy.

Reframe what you are looking at. A review is not a private argument between you and one unhappy client. It is a public document, and the real audience is the next 200 people deciding whether to book you. You are never really writing to the reviewer. You are writing to the silent crowd reading over their shoulder, and that crowd rewards composure and punishes defensiveness. Keep them in mind and the right words come easily.

On your own site this matters less, because BookReady reviews are verified to real bookings and owner-moderated, so you are rarely blindsided by a total stranger. Google, Yelp, and the rest still let anyone post, and those are the ones that need a steady hand. If you run a hair salon, this steady approach is the difference between a profile that reassures and one that scares people off.

Framework

The three-step response framework.

Every good reply does the same three things in the same order. Learn it once and you will never freeze over a review again.

Step 1, acknowledge. Thank them for the feedback and name the specific issue they raised, without excuses. Not "sorry you feel that way," which reads as dismissive, but "you are right that your color came out darker than we discussed." Naming the real problem proves you actually read it and you are not hiding behind a template. That alone disarms most of the tension.

Step 2, take it offline. Invite them to a direct channel, a phone number or an email, to make it right. This does two jobs at once: it shows you genuinely want to fix things, and it moves the messy back-and-forth out of the public thread where every future client would otherwise watch it unfold.

Step 3, never argue publicly. Do not relitigate the facts, correct their version of events, or defend yourself at length in the open. Even when you are completely right, arguing makes you look defensive, and the silent audience sides with the calm party.

A model reply that hits all three: "Thank you for telling us, and I am sorry your balayage came out brassier than you wanted. That is not the result we aim for. I would love to have you back to correct it at no charge. Please call us at [PHONE] or email [EMAIL] and we will get you booked." Three sentences, all three steps, zero defensiveness. That is the whole move.

Don't

What not to do (the moves that make it worse).

Most reputation damage is self-inflicted in the reply box. Here are the six mistakes that reliably make a bad review worse, and why each one backfires.

1. Arguing the facts in public. "That is not what happened, you were 25 minutes late." You might be right, and it still makes you look defensive and small. Nobody scrolling ever sides with the business that is keeping score.

2. Getting personal or sarcastic. One clever, cutting line, one screenshot, and it follows you around forever. Sarcasm always reads as cruelty to the people who were not in the room.

3. Sharing private details to discredit them. Revealing their appointment history, what they paid, or anything health-related to prove they are wrong can violate privacy rules and reads as genuinely cruel. Never do it, no matter how tempting.

4. Copy-paste corporate apologies. "We value your feedback and strive for excellence." It sounds hollow the first time and worse when a prospect sees the identical line under five different reviews. Write like a person.

5. Begging for removal in the reply. "Please take this down, it is hurting my business." It signals the review got to you and hands the reviewer power over you in public.

6. Going silent on a serious complaint. Ignoring a substantive review looks like you do not care, or worse, cannot answer. Silence is its own reply, and it is a bad one.

The win

Turning a one-star into a rebooking.

Here is the part owners miss when they are panicking: a well-handled complaint often does more for you than a spotless record would. A flawless five-star profile can read as either fake or lucky. A profile where something went wrong and you visibly made it right reads as trustworthy, because that is the thing every prospect is actually worried about. What happens when I am the one who is unhappy?

So walk the play. Respond calmly in public using the framework. Then resolve generously in private: a redo, a partial refund, or a genuinely thoughtful fix that costs you a little and buys back a lot. Overdeliver on the recovery, because the recovery is the marketing. Once the client is happy again, and only then, it is fair to mention you would welcome an updated review. Say it lightly, never demand it, and never make the fix conditional on a better rating.

A surprising share of removed or upgraded reviews come from exactly this sequence: calm reply, generous private fix, gentle follow-up. And notice what you have really done. You did not just clean up a review, you recovered a client who was on their way out the door. That is a retention story, and retention is where the money is. This is win-back in miniature, so it pairs naturally with your client retention playbook and the automated win-back tools that bring lapsed clients back on their own.

Examples

Response examples for the most common complaints.

Steal these. Each one uses the framework, keeps to three or four sentences, and offers an offline next step. Swap the [BRACKETED] fields for your own. They work on Google, Yelp, or anywhere public.

1. Service result complaint

"Thank you for the honest feedback, and I am sorry your color came out darker than we talked through. That is on us to get right. I would love to have you back to correct it at no charge, please call [PHONE] or email [EMAIL] and we will book you in this week."

2. Wait-time or running-late complaint

"You are right, we ran behind that afternoon and your time matters. I am sorry we kept you waiting. I would like to make it up to you on your next visit, please reach me directly at [PHONE] so I can set that up personally."

3. Price or fee surprise

"Thank you for flagging this, and I am sorry the final total caught you off guard. That should have been clear before we started. I would like to walk through it with you and make it right, please email [EMAIL] and I will follow up the same day."

4. A vague one-star with no detail

"I am sorry your visit missed the mark, and I would genuinely like to understand what happened so we can fix it. Would you email me at [EMAIL] or call [PHONE]? I read every message myself and I want the chance to make this right."

Notice none of them argue, and all of them move the conversation offline. Once you are collecting reviews at volume, keep a matching set of positive-ask templates handy too, the ones in our guide to getting more salon reviews pair perfectly with these.

Fakes

When it is fake or defamatory, dispute instead of reply.

Not every bad review deserves a thoughtful response. Some deserve a report and a removal, and treating those two categories the same is a mistake. Replying earnestly to a fake only lends it credibility and keeps it near the top of your profile longer.

Learn the signs of a flaggable review. It describes a service you do not offer, names the wrong city, or is clearly meant for a different business. It comes from a competitor or a banned ex-employee with an axe to grind. It contains threats or outright extortion, the "pay me or this stays up" kind. It uses slurs, or it makes specific, provably false claims about what you did. Those are not feedback. They are violations of the platform's own rules.

The path is different too. Do not engage emotionally in public, because a fight is exactly what a bad-faith poster wants. Instead, report the review through the platform's flagging tools and attach evidence: no booking on record, a matching name from a known dispute, screenshots of any threats. If it is genuinely defamatory and causing real, measurable damage, that has crossed into a legal question and is worth a conversation with an attorney. One built-in advantage: BookReady reviews are verified against real completed bookings, so drive-by fakes simply cannot post on your own booking page. You cannot verify Google or Yelp the same way, which is exactly why flagging matters there.

Get ahead

The best defense: more recent, honest reviews.

A single bad review stings most when it sits near the top of a thin profile. If you have eleven reviews and one is a one-star, that one is 9% of your reputation and it is the first thing a hesitant client reads. The problem is not really the review. The problem is that it has nowhere to hide.

So bury it the honest way. Collect a steady, ongoing stream of fresh verified reviews so a single off day becomes one quiet voice among many recent ones. When you have a constant drip of new five-stars, an old one-star drops down the page and loses its power to define you, and it does it without any tricks. Owners who ask after every visit almost never panic over a single bad review, because they know three good ones are landing this week.

The trick is to make the ask automatic instead of relying on memory. Work through the get-more-reviews playbook, then let BookReady's reviews feature fire a verified request after every completed appointment on its own. You focus on the chair. The profile keeps refreshing itself in the background, which is the whole point.

Protect it

Building a reputation that survives a bad day.

Zoom out and the whole thing stops feeling fragile. Reputation is not a score you defend one review at a time, it is a system you run. Four moving parts: collect reviews automatically so the flow never depends on you remembering, moderate your own-site display so you control what shows next to the book button, respond to the public ones in the same calm voice every single time, and resolve problems in the chair before they ever reach a review at all.

The last one does the most work. Most one-stars are avoidable friction that surfaced too late, a confusing price, a rushed consult, a result nobody flagged in the room. Catch those live and half your bad reviews never get written.

There is one durable advantage worth building deliberately. Verified, owner-moderated reviews on your own booking page are an asset you actually own, one that does not vanish when a public platform quietly changes its algorithm or its rules. That is yours for good. Handle the rare bad review with a steady hand, and let the system do the rest.

Questions

The short answers.

Should I respond to every negative review?

Respond to every genuine complaint, calmly and in public, because future clients judge your reply more than they judge the review itself. Fake or defamatory ones are the exception: report and remove those instead of arguing with them in the open.

What should I say in response to a bad review?

Thank them, name the specific issue without making excuses, and invite them to a direct channel to make it right. Keep it to three or four sentences and never argue the facts in public. The calm tone does the persuading, not the defense.

How fast should I reply to a negative review?

Within a day or two, but never in the first angry ten minutes. Wait an hour, cool off, and reread it. Then write for the silent audience of future clients rather than for the reviewer, and the reply almost writes itself.

Can I get a fake or defamatory review removed?

Often, yes. Report reviews that are clearly not from a real client, come from competitors or banned ex-staff, contain threats or slurs, or make provably false claims. Use the platform's flagging tools and attach evidence. Genuine defamation that causes real damage is a legal question.

Should I offer a refund to fix a bad review?

Sometimes. A redo or a partial refund can recover the client and impress everyone watching. Resolve generously in private first. Once the client is happy again, it is fair to welcome an updated review, but never demand one as a condition of the fix.

What should I never do when responding to a bad review?

Never argue the facts publicly, get sarcastic, share private appointment or health details, or beg for the review to be taken down inside your reply. Each of those reads worse to future clients than the original complaint ever did.

Do BookReady reviews protect me from fake reviews?

On your own booking page, yes. BookReady reviews are verified to real completed bookings and owner-moderated, so drive-by fakes cannot post there. Public sites like Google and Yelp still allow anyone to post, so those continue to need flagging when something is not legitimate.

How do I stop one bad review from dominating my profile?

Collect a steady stream of recent, honest reviews so one off day becomes one voice among many. Automating the ask after every completed visit keeps the profile fresh, which naturally pushes a single one-star down and out of the spotlight.
